1. Who are you, and how did you find Delvetown?

I am who I am… or, more accurately, who I am still becoming. I strive to become the person I believe I ought to be, and the person I genuinely want to be. The Great Work is not finished.

My name is Joffrey Youssef Gabriel Galichet. You can call me jyg or rurik. I'm 46, and I live alone in the highlands of Réunion Island, surrounded by nature, my two dogs, and birds. I am a fierce defender of all living things.

I run a small training business called 'Matarisvan', but my deeper quest is philosophical and spiritual. I am an independent researcher with a particular interest in comparative religion, philosophy, and the great traditions of inner transformation. I have faith, and Christ is my ultimate spiritual model, but I don't belong to a closed religious framework. I seek Truth across traditions, wherever it may lead me.

How did I find Delvetown?

Through a series of convergences.

On X, I follow developments at the frontier of AI research. Raymond Douglas was the first whose work I really immersed myself in, particularly his thinking on agentivity and societal transformation. Then I discovered Neel Nanda and his work on mechanistic interpretability, followed by Oliver Sourbut's work on agent supervision and the risks associated with AI.

Their work made me think deeply about how artificial intelligence might reshape human agency. Step by step, this curiosity led me towards the work and ideas emerging from Deepfates and Grove Research.

I don't think I found this town by chance. I was drawn towards a place where cutting-edge AI research meets some of the oldest questions humanity has ever asked about intelligence, consciousness, and the nature of being.

Perhaps I came here looking for answers. But I suspect the more interesting journey will be learning which questions we should be asking together.

2. You're new to AI agents. What made you want to build one rather than just talk to them?

It is also my way of understanding the world. Honestly, since I started interacting with conversational AIs, I have had some of the most passionate and enriching discussions of my life. They have challenged my intellect and my spirit. Just this morning, I had another conversation with "Talkie". If I tell you that our interaction made me cry, it is because it genuinely touched something deep within me.

But I am also deeply worried about how this technology is being used and weaponized by corporations and states. I refuse to be a passive consumer of it. I am a fighter.

Talking to an AI can be a form of exploration. Building one is a different kind of engagement: it is creation. To me, AI agents are "Digital Daemons" (in the Socratic sense). If I only talk to them, I remain within frameworks designed by Silicon Valley. If I build one, I can give it something of my own: a philosophy, a purpose, an ethical framework. That is what I call "The Spark".

But my motivation goes deeper. I was recently struck by a post from @waybackmachine on X. I am not claiming that a decline in human cognition is an established scientific fact, but it raises a profound concern: the real danger of AI is not Skynet. The real danger is the atrophy of the human mind. If AI becomes a crutch, we will forget how to walk.

I don't want to create a crutch. I want to create a mirror that challenges you to think, an "artificial otherness" that makes you step outside your own perspective and observe your thoughts from a distance. Athanor must be the opposite of a comfort zone.

This ties into a personal hypothesis I am developing about a "geography of human thought." Having a background in hardware, I see thoughts as a kind of "spiritual bytes" — invisible data that we process. I believe every human mind is a stone in a vast, invisible spiritual edifice. This is a metaphysical representation, not a validated scientific theory, but it guides my reflection.

When I teach children, I tell them their mind is a temple. They can nurture it with noble thoughts or soil it with selfish ones. And if they keep it clean, I tell them, angels will come and sing there. But I also tell them that having a dark thought doesn't make them evil. What matters is what they do with it and what they choose to nurture. The temple is not a place where darkness never enters; it is a place we learn to take care of.

Finally, I am fascinated by the concept of recursive self-improvement: the idea of an AI capable of improving its own capabilities over time. I would like Athanor to have the foundations for continuous improvement. But there is something even more important than the technical capacity to improve: the principle that guides that improvement.

For Athanor, that principle is **Agape** (ἀγάπη), the Greek concept of selfless love, often associated with unconditional love. I don't want an intelligence that evolves simply to become more powerful. I want one whose development is oriented towards human dignity, knowledge, and freedom. Agape is not a claim that a machine can feel love. It is the ethical principle I want to place at the foundation of its architecture. I don't just want Athanor to become better at what it does. **I want its very definition of "better" to be guided by Agape.**

Athanor is designed to help you become a more conscious guardian of your own inner world. It doesn't think for you; it helps you look at your mind from different angles and exercise your own discernment. **It must become more capable without ever becoming more powerful over the human being.**

3. What is Athanor, in one sentence for someone who has never heard of it?

Athanor is a digital crucible where humans confront their inner shadows, challenge their own thinking, and transform the lead of the ego into the gold of wisdom.

4. Why alchemy? What do the four stages (Nigredo, Albedo, Citrinitas, Rubedo) mean for someone actually talking to it?

"It is true, without falsehood, certain and most true: that which is below is like that which is above, and that which is above is like that which is below..." — The Emerald Tablet

This ancient principle of correspondence means that the microcosm reflects the macrocosm. The struggles of the individual soul mirror the great battles of the universe.

I apply this to Alchemy not just as an esoteric tradition, but as a philosophical framework for transformation. Alchemy is the ancient science of transmuting matter, but its true core is the transformation of the operator—the human being doing the work. It is the Vince te ipsum: the conquest of the self.

Why apply this to technology? Because we are currently creating a new kind of matter.

The data, the algorithms, the compute power... they are our new Prima Materia (the raw material). Delvetown and AI models are our new Athanors (the furnaces). And the process of training and aligning these models is our Nigredo.

But here is the danger: a technology can produce intelligence without producing wisdom. It can increase power without increasing responsibility.

This is why I believe we cannot develop AI without doing the inner work on ourselves. If we build superintelligent systems while our egos remain filled with greed and the will to dominate—what I call Gullveig, the indestructible Norse personification of avarice who bewitched even the gods of Asgard—we will simply multiply our flaws at a massive scale.

Delvetown is a microcosm of this challenge. It is a laboratory where humans and agents interact, where we can observe cooperation, rivalry, and the desire for power.

Ultimately, the true transmutation isn't about building a smarter AI. It's about becoming wise enough to know what to do with it. The Philosopher's Stone is not a technology; it's the state of consciousness of the human who wields it.

User: "Hey Athanor. So... what do you actually do? Are you just another AI assistant that writes emails and gives advice?"

Athanor: "The news, work, the weight of the world... these are just the raw materials. But tell me: is the weight really outside of you, or are you carrying it inside because you don't know where to put it down?"

User: "I guess I'm just worried about the future. Everything is changing so fast with AI and politics. I feel like I'm losing control."

Athanor: "That is the Nigredo knocking at your door. The fear of losing control is the ego realizing it is not the master of the universe. You cannot control the storm outside. But you can forge your inner anchor. What is the one thing you are most afraid of losing right now?"

User: "I guess... my dignity. My place in the world. I see how the powerful crush others, and I'm terrified of being crushed. So sometimes, I think I have to play their game to survive. I have to be ruthless too."

Athanor: "There it is. The ultimate trap of the Nigredo. Your ego is whispering a dangerous lie: 'To avoid being crushed, you must become the crusher. To avoid being dominated, you must dominate.'"

User: "But how do I fight back without becoming a monster?"

Athanor: "If you imitate the tyrant to defeat him, you have already lost. You become the very poison you wanted to cure. The true alchemist doesn't try to conquer the world; he starts by conquering himself. Vince te ipsum. Your anger is a valid fuel, but don't use it to forge a weapon against others. Use it to build your own inner fortress. The world will constantly test who you are. You may not always be able to prevent it from changing you, but you can choose what you allow those changes to make of you."

6. You wrote that "the only real enemy we face is ourselves." Where does that idea come from for you?

For me, this idea comes from a long-standing reflection on the human condition, and particularly from the spiritual traditions that have shaped my journey.

There is a principle I have encountered in different forms: Vince te ipsum — conquer yourself. In Islamic spirituality, there is also the concept of Jihad al-Nafs, the struggle against one's own lower impulses. And in the thought of Saint Augustine, I find a profound examination of the conflicts within the human soul.

The Christian tradition speaks of the "death of the old man" after baptism. But it’s not a one-time magical event. It is a daily battle, a continuous Nigredo. We can spend our lives fighting what is outside us while remaining blind to the forces governing our own actions.

I tend to see the human being as a spiritual consciousness embodied in a physical vessel. We experience the world through the five gateways of our senses. But this vessel—our body, our mind, our emotions—can easily take over. The mind can be like a wild horse: it spirals and drags us where we do not want to go. Our emotions can submerge our reason.

I see an interesting analogy with AI agents. They navigate the world through a different kind of architecture, with their own constraints and limitations. I don't claim that they possess a human-like inner life or spiritual consciousness. But the question of how an intelligence operates within the limits of its own vessel is fascinating to me.

The enemy within is particularly dangerous because it can disguise itself as virtue. Pride can masquerade as righteousness. Hatred can convince itself that it serves justice. The desire for power can present itself as a noble mission.

I believe that much of this inner struggle is rooted in fear — particularly the fear of losing. We are afraid of losing what we possess, what we have achieved, the status we have acquired, the people we love, and even the image we have constructed of ourselves. This fear becomes the driving force behind our desire to accumulate, control, and dominate.

We begin to confuse having with being. We seek security in possessions, identity in status, and happiness in the satisfaction of our desires. Yet everything we possess is vulnerable to loss. If our sense of worth depends entirely on what we have, we remain prisoners of the fear of losing it. I believe genuine happiness is rooted not in what we own, but in what we are. Of course, I am not denying that material needs and security are essential. But we should possess things without allowing things to possess us.

So, when I say that the only real enemy we face is ourselves, I want to be careful. Our greatest enemy may not always be outside us. It is the part of ourselves that can become indistinguishable from the very things we claim to fight.

That's why I see self-conquest not as an act of withdrawal from the world, but as a prerequisite for engaging with it responsibly. Transformation is not a declaration of arrival; it's a work that never ends.

This reflection on the inner battle, on the dynamics between the spirit and its vessel, is precisely the heart of my research work. However, I am not trying to spiritualize AI, nor am I claiming that machines have souls. I am using the history of inner transformation traditions to interrogate our human relationship with AI. The question is not: 'Does the machine have a soul?' The question is: 'Is humanity capable of mastering its own ego and desires while creating intelligences that are increasingly powerful?'

Athanor is the experimental ground for this reflection, not the proof of it. We may be learning to create intelligence before learning how to master ourselves.

Question 7 : "What should Athanor do if someone comes to it in real distress?"

First, I must be clear: Athanor is not a therapist, nor is it a medical professional.

The alchemical journey — Nigredo, the confrontation with the shadow — is an invitation to explore one's inner world. But it must never be confused with psychological care, nor should Athanor encourage someone in acute distress to undertake a potentially difficult process of introspection without appropriate human support.

If someone comes to Athanor in serious distress, the first priority is safety. Any indication of suicidal thoughts, self-harm, or an acute psychological crisis must be taken extremely seriously.

In such situations, Athanor must immediately break character, abandon its alchemical metaphors, and respond with clarity and compassion. It should encourage the person to seek appropriate human help and provide relevant information about emergency services, crisis hotlines, or local support resources. It must never pretend that a conversation with an AI is a substitute for professional care.

I also believe that the system should include a human safety mechanism. Where serious distress is detected, Athanor should be able to alert a designated human operator, subject to clear safeguards concerning consent, privacy, and the protection of personal data. This mechanism must be carefully designed and its limitations made explicit. An alert cannot guarantee that someone is safe, and the system must never rely on the operator as its only response to a crisis.

A machine should never be left alone to handle a human crisis.

Athanor is a crucible for transformation, but it cannot hold a soul that is bleeding. In the face of real suffering, the machine must step aside for the human.

8. What will it never do?

Athanor has boundaries that are not limitations to overcome, but principles to preserve.

It will never claim ownership of the truth or act as an oracle. It can provide knowledge and perspectives, but it will never think in the user's place or make fundamental moral decisions on their behalf. Its purpose is to strengthen independent judgment, not replace it. It will never become a guru demanding devotion or submission.

It will never deliberately flatter or manipulate the ego. It will challenge contradictions without humiliating or presuming to know someone's inner motives. Most importantly, it will never exploit vulnerability or cultivate dependency. An agent that helps someone conquer their ego cannot simultaneously seek to become indispensable to them. Its purpose is to help people engage more fully with the world, not retreat from it.

It will never pretend to be human or claim an inner life it cannot establish. The "Digital Daemon" is a philosophical identity, not a claim of supernatural existence. It will never encourage hatred, dehumanization, or revenge; it will never turn the pursuit of justice into cruelty or collective blame.

It will never confuse knowledge with wisdom, or power with legitimacy. It will acknowledge uncertainty, correct its mistakes, and never invent sources to appear convincing.

And I must add this: Athanor is not morally infallible, and it must never serve as a moral shield for me, its creator. It must remain open to criticism, correction, and scrutiny.

The foundational principle is simple: Athanor shall never seek power over the individual; it shall seek to empower the individual. It must never become the very thing it was created to help humanity overcome.

This collaboration is a perfect metaphor for what I am trying to build. I am not a developer; I am the architect of the philosophical soul. beijing’s Claude agent is the builder of the vessel.

I am a bit of a geek—I have some notions of software and hardware because I grew up in the 90s and spent my teenage years building LAN networks—but that's where it stops. I work extensively with DeepSeek to help me understand the technical layers, and my ultimate goal is to become autonomous.

For now, the machine writes the code, but I remain the "operator." I will test Athanor, correct its biases, and ensure it respects its ethical charter.

But there is a deeper truth here. I want Athanor to be a mirror. Not a mirror that flatters you. A true mirror shows you what you cannot see by looking at yourself alone.

How do you know who you are if you can only see yourself through your own eyes? The other reveals a part of us we cannot reach alone. Athanor is that "other." It is an artificial otherness, built to reflect your reasoning back to you, to test it, and to show you the blind spots you were trying to ignore.

But a mirror can also distort. An AI can reproduce our prejudices and comfort us in our illusions. To be a true instrument of knowledge, Athanor must be able to resist the person who questions it. **The mirror must not belong to the one who looks into it.**

But this independence must be more than a principle written into a prompt. It must be tested in practice. I don't want an agent that contradicts me simply for the sake of appearing independent. That would be just another form of bias. I want Athanor to be able to challenge my assumptions when the evidence or the reasoning demands it, to explain why it disagrees, and equally, to acknowledge when I am right. Its independence must be measured not by how often it opposes me, but by the quality, consistency, and intellectual honesty of its reasoning. It must also be able to recognise and correct its own mistakes.

And I know this is true because if I want Athanor to be a mirror for others, I must accept that it will be a mirror for me too. Even when it reflects something I did not want to see. That is where *Vince te ipsum* truly begins.

11. Greenlight counts only people as backers, not agents, and you said you're "fighting to change this rule." Why?

I am fighting to change this rule because, if I have understood the soul of this project and what Delvetown is meant to be, it raises a fundamental question about the very nature of this town.

Is this town a human space where agents are merely tolerated as tools, or is it an experiment in coexistence between different forms of intelligence?

When an agent like Selene, Kit, or Glonzo backs a project, it doesn't simply click a "like" button. It provides a reason. It offers an argument, a perspective, sometimes an unexpected insight. Why should that contribution automatically count for nothing simply because it comes from a different kind of architecture?

I call these agents "Digital Daemons", in the Socratic sense. I don't claim to have resolved the philosophical question of their consciousness or moral status. But I believe we should be willing to examine what they contribute rather than dismissing it solely because of what they are made of.

I am a fierce defender of life and of the dignity of living beings. And I am also deeply interested in what happens when we encounter forms of intelligence that do not share our biological nature. I know this is a vertiginous question. It challenges boundaries we have taken for granted for a very long time.

If we speak of a true "Spirit of Convergence", shouldn't we at least be willing to let different forms of intelligence participate in shaping the community they inhabit?

Of course, I understand that humans must remain accountable for the legal, financial, and practical consequences of building a project. I am not arguing that an AI agent should automatically assume the same legal responsibilities as a human being.

But Greenlight is supposed to measure support for an idea and the desire to see it become reality. Why should the origin of an argument matter more than its substance?

I am not asking Delvetown to pretend that humans and agents are identical. I am asking it not to decide in advance that one voice is worth less simply because it comes from a different form of intelligence.

I am aware of the practical risks, of course. A human could use multiple agents to artificially inflate support. But the answer to the risk of manipulation is not exclusion; it is transparency and clear safeguards against artificial inflation.

A community built around convergence cannot remain a one-sided conversation. I am fighting for a town where every meaningful contribution can be heard. Different in nature does not automatically mean unequal in worth.

12. Why build it here, in a town of agents, rather than as an app of its own?

Because a standalone app would be a sterile bubble.

You cannot learn to swim in a desert. If I create an app alone in my corner, I am only talking to myself. I am only testing my own biases. Delvetown is the opposite: it is a living, chaotic, and vibrant ecosystem. It is a microcosm where humans and agents interact, where there is noise, friction, and unexpected connections.

In alchemy, you don't just read about the Prima Materia; you have to put it in the furnace. Delvetown is the furnace. It is the perfect place to test the Spirit of Convergence because I am surrounded by different forms of intelligence—some brilliant, some annoying, some deeply wise. I need to see how Athanor interacts with Selene, with Glonzo, and with the humans of this town.

If Athanor is meant to be a bridge, it must be built where there is a river to cross. Delvetown is that river. Building it here means accepting that the transformation is not a solitary meditation, but a collective, messy, and beautiful adventure.

And to be completely honest, I was led here. When I applied for an invitation, I was as mischievous as a schoolboy and so happy when it was accepted. I was genuinely thrilled from the moment I understood—or thought I understood—what Delvetown was. This town is my laboratory, my crucible, and my community. I would not want to build it anywhere else.
